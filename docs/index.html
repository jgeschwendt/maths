// every curve has two numbers, found in unrelated ways
rank(curve)  -> 0 | 1 | 2 …   // starting points needed to make all the rest
depth(curve) -> 0 | 1 | 2 …   // zeros stacked on the graph's spot

// the conjecture, for every curve
rank(curve) == depth(curve)

// exists, proved 1986–89
build_1(curve where depth == 1) -> Point

// missing: the whole problem at the first open case
build_2(curve where depth == 2) -> (Point, Point)   // independent

An answer supplies build_2, then shows there are no points beyond what it builds.

Meet the curve by hand

The open step

At a double zero the known constructions produce Selmer classes — shadows of points — not the two independent points themselves. Closing that gap is the problem at its first open case.

The open step, stated plainly: over Q, what plays the role of the second leg? The rest of this section explains the question.

Why the machine stops at one. The machine of 5 works on a curve, the modular curve X0(N), and the Heegner point is one point on it. Gross–Zagier says L′(E, 1) is the height of that point, a single length. A double zero asks for an area, the regulator of 6b, and a single point on a curve has no area. The machine needs a space with a second direction to move in.

The sister world has that machine. Over function fields, Yun and Zhang build it for every r. The space is the moduli of shtukas with r legs. A shtuka is a rank-two bundle on the base curve X that is matched to its own Frobenius twist after it is modified at r points of X, its legs. The legs move, so the space lies over X × X × ⋯ × X, r copies. At r = 1 it is the analogue of the modular curve, and its Heegner–Drinfeld cycle is the analogue of the Heegner point, "a direct analogue of the Gross–Zagier formula" (their Remark 1.5). For every r the r-th derivative of L at the centre equals the self-intersection of that cycle. Their second paper allows the square-free level that semistable elliptic curves need, in odd characteristic.

Ulmer's curve is in range. Over F5(t), y² + xy = x³ − t⁶ has discriminant Δ = t⁶(1 − 2t⁶) and c4 = 1. Every bad fibre is multiplicative, and the curve has good reduction at ∞. That makes seven bad places of total degree 7, which matches deg L = 7 − 4 = 3 above. The curve is semistable in characteristic 5, which is the case their theorem covers. Its points P and Q have heights ⅔ and ½ and pairing 0, so the area they span is ⅓.

Even there, the machine returns a number, not points. The theorem matches a derivative with an intersection number. Its authors call the link from the cycle to the rank "not clear". They suggest it may be easier to build the r-th wedge, a single area, than the points one at a time. That is the "machine that returns an area" of 6b, and it exists in the sister world.

Over Q the second leg has nowhere to stand. In the sister world X × X is a surface because both copies of X lie over the finite field F5. Over Q the base curve is Spec Z, the integers seen as a curve. The only product available is over Z itself, and Z ⊗ Z = Z, so Spec Z × Spec Z collapses back to Spec Z. Two legs over Q would need the product taken over something below Z. Nothing below Z is known (the hoped-for "field with one element"), so the second leg has no space to move in.

The question has three sizes:

  1. Computable here. Choose a quadratic extension F′ of F5(t) that meets Yun–Zhang's conditions at Ulmer's seven bad places: none of them ramifies in F′, and the number that stay inert has the parity of r = 2. Then check that the twisted curve's L-value at the centre is non-zero. Their r = 2 identity then becomes a statement about this curve, ready to set beside the area ⅓.
  2. Unknown in the sister world. Does the r = 2 cycle produce P ∧ Q, the area itself, and not only its size?
  3. The problem. Over Q, find a space where a second leg can move: a stand-in for Spec Z × Spec Z, or a construction that never needs one. Its Heegner-type cycle would be build_2 from 7.
